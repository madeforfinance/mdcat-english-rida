2.7 — Positions of Adverbs

What is an adverb?

An adverb modifies a verb, adjective, another adverb, a phrase or sometimes an entire sentence. It can express manner, place, time, frequency, degree, certainty, viewpoint or connection.

KindQuestion answeredExamples
MannerHow?carefully, quickly, silently, badly
PlaceWhere?here, there, outside, everywhere
TimeWhen?now, soon, yesterday, recently
FrequencyHow often?always, usually, often, rarely, never
DegreeTo what extent?very, quite, too, almost, extremely
CertaintyHow certain?certainly, probably, perhaps, definitely
ViewpointFrom what perspective?personally, financially, politically
Linking/conjunctiveHow does this idea connect?however, therefore, moreover, consequently

Three main positions

Front position

At the beginning of a clause, often for emphasis or framing.

Yesterday, we revised grammar.

Mid position

Usually around the auxiliary or main verb.

She often revises at night.

End position

Often after the object or at the end of the clause.

She answered the question carefully.

Adverbs of frequency

Verb structureTypical positionExample
With ordinary main verbBefore the main verbHe usually studies early.
With beAfter beShe is always punctual.
With auxiliary/modalAfter the first auxiliaryThey have never complained.
For emphasisFront or end positionSometimes, I study at dawn.
Roman Urdu trick: Frequency adverbs ko “verb ke guard” samjhein. Normal verb se pehle, “be” ke baad, aur helping verb ke baad aate hain: He often goes. He is often late. He has often gone.

Adverb position can change meaning

SentenceMeaning/focus
Only Ali solved the problem.No one except Ali solved it.
Ali only solved the problem.He solved it but did little else.
Ali solved only the problem.He solved the problem and not other tasks.
Even Sara understood the rule.Sara was an unexpected person to understand it.

Order of adverbs

A common neutral order at the end of a sentence is:

Manner → Place → Time
She worked quietly in the library yesterday.

Frequency and certainty adverbs often occur in mid-position, while manner, place and time commonly appear later. This is a tendency, not an unbreakable law.

Important distinctions

  • Hard means with effort; hardly means scarcely. “He works hard” ≠ “He hardly works.”
  • Late means not on time; lately means recently.
  • Near indicates distance; nearly means almost.
  • High indicates great height; highly means very or greatly.
  • Good is usually an adjective; well is usually an adverb, though “well” can also be an adjective meaning healthy.

Common adverb errors

Incorrect: She speaks English fluent.
Correct: She speaks English fluently.
Incorrect: He goes always to college.
Better: He always goes to college.
Incorrect: I very like this book.
Correct: I like this book very much.
Memory line: “Only” ko jis word se attach karna ho, us ke bilkul qareeb rakhein. Warna sentence ka meaning quietly escape kar jata hai.

Movement and direction

High-yield contrasts

  • In a room = location; into a room = movement.
  • On the table = location; onto the table = movement.
  • Across the road = crossing from one side to another; along the road = following its length.
  • Between is not limited to exactly two in every modern usage, but it is especially natural for distinct, individually identified relationships.
  • By car describes means of transport; in the car describes being inside the vehicle.

2.9 — Transitional Devices

Transitional devices connect ideas and show the logical relationship between sentences, clauses and paragraphs. They improve coherence and guide the reader through an argument.

PurposeUseful transitionsExample
Additionalso, moreover, furthermore, in additionThe plan is affordable; moreover, it is practical.
Contrasthowever, nevertheless, in contrast, on the other handThe task was difficult; however, we completed it.
Concessionalthough, even though, admittedly, neverthelessAlthough it was late, they continued.
Causebecause, since, as, owing toBecause it rained, the match was delayed.
Resulttherefore, thus, consequently, as a resultIt rained; therefore, the match was delayed.
Sequencefirst, next, then, subsequently, finallyFirst read the question; then analyse the options.
Examplefor example, for instance, such asSome metals, such as iron, are magnetic.
Clarificationin other words, that is, namelyThe rule is restrictive; in other words, it limits choice.
Comparisonsimilarly, likewise, in the same wayBoth methods are efficient; likewise, both are economical.
Emphasisindeed, above all, especially, notablyThe evidence is especially important.
Conclusionin conclusion, overall, to sum up, ultimatelyOverall, the proposal is viable.

Punctuation with transitional adverbs

When however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless connect two independent clauses, a semicolon before and a comma after is a safe formal pattern.

The evidence was limited; however, the conclusion was reasonable.

Do not use a comma alone to join two independent clauses.

Incorrect: The evidence was limited, however, the conclusion was reasonable.
Correct: The evidence was limited; however, the conclusion was reasonable.

Conjunction vs conjunctive adverb

Coordinating conjunctionConjunctive adverb
and, but, so, yethowever, therefore, moreover, consequently
Two clauses may be joined with a comma + conjunction.Use a semicolon or separate sentences when joining independent clauses.
Roman Urdu trick: Transition ko “road sign” samjhein. Addition bataye to moreover, ulta relation ho to however, reason ho to because, result ho to therefore. Har jagah “and” chipkana writing ko traffic jam bana deta hai.

Comma rules that frequently appear in exams

  • Use a comma after an introductory dependent clause: “When the bell rang, the students left.”
  • Use commas around non-essential information: “My brother, who lives in Lahore, is visiting.”
  • Do not put a comma between a subject and its main verb: “The students in the hall are ready.”
  • Use commas in a series: “Books, pens, files and folders.” The final Oxford comma depends on style.
  • Use a comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses: “I revised, and I practised.”
  • Use a comma after a direct address: “Asad, please read the sentence.”

Semicolon vs colon

Semicolon: Both sides are independent clauses.
“The question was difficult; the explanation was clear.”
Colon: The first part is complete and introduces what follows.
“She brought the essentials: a notebook, a pen and a ruler.”

Apostrophe rules

RuleExample
Singular possessionthe student's book
Plural ending in sthe students' books
Irregular plural possessionchildren's toys
Contractionit is → it's; do not → don't
Its vs it'sIts colour is blue. / It's cold.

Quotation marks and reported speech

Direct speech: Sara said, “I am ready.”
Reported speech: Sara said that she was ready.

Quotation punctuation conventions vary between American and British styles. Follow the style specified by the question or textbook, and keep the quotation boundaries clear.

Hyphen, dash and minus sign

  • Hyphen: joins words: “part-time job,” “well-known author.”
  • En dash: often indicates ranges or relationships: “pages 10–15,” “Pakistan–China relations.”
  • Em dash: creates a strong break: “The answer—despite its simplicity—was correct.”
  • Minus sign: mathematical operation, not ordinary punctuation.
Exam caution: Punctuation rules have style variations. Focus on the grammatical function, sentence structure and the style used in your examination material rather than memorising one publisher's typography as universal law.

Integrated Editing Practice

“However the instructions were clear the candidates often ignored them therefore the supervisor repeated them slowly and carefully.”

Improved version: “However, although the instructions were clear, the candidates often ignored them; therefore, the supervisor repeated them slowly and carefully.”

C. “For” expresses duration; “since” would introduce a starting point such as “since 2020.”

B. “Across” expresses movement from one side to the other.

10. “He walked ______ the room” indicates movement from outside to inside.

A. “Into” expresses movement toward the inside.

11. Which transition best shows a result? “The roads were flooded; ______, the school closed.”

D. “Consequently” introduces the result of the flooding.

12. Which transition best expresses contrast? “The method is simple; ______, it is highly effective.”

B. “Nevertheless” signals an unexpected contrast or concession.

13. Which punctuation correctly joins two independent clauses?

C. A semicolon can join closely related independent clauses.

14. Choose the correctly punctuated sentence.

A. A comma commonly follows an introductory phrase.

15. Which sentence uses a colon correctly?

D. The clause before the colon is complete and introduces a list.

16. Choose the correct possessive form for a book belonging to several students.

B. A plural noun ending in s generally takes an apostrophe after the s.

17. Which sentence correctly distinguishes “its” and “it's”?

C. “Its” shows possession; “it's” means “it is.”

18. Which punctuation mark is most suitable for a strong interruption in a sentence?

A. An em dash can signal a strong break or interruption.

19. Which sentence avoids a comma splice?

D. A semicolon separates the independent clauses, and commas set off “however.”

20. Which sentence has the clearest adverb placement?

B. The placement makes “only” modify “the truth,” although context may require another arrangement.

Final One-Page Revision

  • Adverb position depends on kind, verb structure, emphasis and intended meaning.
  • Frequency adverbs usually come before ordinary main verbs, after “be,” and after the first auxiliary.
  • Neutral end order is commonly manner → place → time.
  • AT = exact point/time; ON = days, dates and surfaces; IN = larger periods and interiors.
  • Into/onto show movement; in/on usually show position.
  • Across means from one side to another; along means following a route or line.
  • Transitions show addition, contrast, cause, result, sequence, example and conclusion.
  • Do not join two independent clauses with only a comma.
  • Semicolon joins related independent clauses; colon introduces an explanation or list after a complete clause.
  • Apostrophe shows possession or contraction. “Its” is possessive; “it's” means “it is.”
