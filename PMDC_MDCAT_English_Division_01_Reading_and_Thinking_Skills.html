Outcome 1.1 — Scan to Answer Short Questions

1. Meaning of Scanning

Scanning is a reading technique used to locate a particular piece of information without reading every word of a passage.

You scan for names, dates, figures, places, technical terms, causes, results, definitions, and other specific details.

Roman Urdu trick: Scanning ka matlab poora passage ratta lagana nahi. Pehle decide karein ke question mein kya dhoondna hai, phir sirf us information ki location find karein.

2. Scanning, Skimming and Intensive Reading

MethodPurposeTypical target
ScanningFind a specific detailWho, when, where, how many, which year
SkimmingUnderstand the general ideaTitle, topic sentence, conclusion, overall message
Intensive readingUnderstand deeplyArgument, evidence, implication, tone, relationships
Memory rule: Scan = Specific. Skim = Summary. Intensive reading = In-depth understanding.

3. The Q-L-S-C Method

Q — Question

Read the question first and identify the exact information required.

L — Locate

Choose strong keywords such as names, numbers, unusual nouns and technical terms.

S — Search variations

Search synonyms, paraphrases, abbreviations and grammatical alternatives.

C — Confirm

Read the complete sentence and nearby context before selecting an answer.

Roman Urdu: Keyword mil jana final answer nahi hota. Keyword ke aas-paas ka sentence zaroor parhein, warna aik lafz dekh kar ghalat conclusion nikal sakte hain.

6. Numbers, Dates, Names and Units

  • Look for digits, percentages, years, dates and measurements.
  • Notice capitalized names, places and institutions.
  • Check the unit beside every number.
  • Distinguish the date of discovery, publication, approval and implementation.
Trap: “20” alone is incomplete. It may mean 20 people, 20%, 20 years or 20 cases. The unit is part of the answer.

8. Distractor Analysis

  1. Same-word trap: repeats a word but changes its meaning.
  2. Half-true option: one part is correct and one part is unsupported.
  3. Extreme wording: always, never, completely, only.
  4. Reversed relationship: effect is presented as the cause.
  5. Wrong reference: a pronoun is connected to the wrong noun.
  6. Outside knowledge: generally true but absent from the passage.
Golden rule: Select the option supported by the text, not the option that merely sounds intelligent.
Worked example: “The treatment reduced symptoms but did not eliminate the disease.”
Supported: The treatment reduced symptoms without eliminating the disease.
Not supported: The treatment cured every patient, or the treatment had no effect.

Outcome 1.2 — Deduce the Meanings from the Context

1. What Does “Meaning from Context” Mean?

To deduce meaning from context is to infer the meaning of an unfamiliar word or expression by examining the surrounding words, sentences, examples, contrasts, and overall situation.

Roman Urdu: Agar word unknown ho, foran dictionary ki taraf na bhaagein. Pehle dekhein sentence us word ke bare mein kya clue de raha hai.

2. Main Types of Context Clues

Definition clue

The meaning is directly explained.

A recluse, a person who avoids society, lived alone.

Example clue

Examples reveal the category or meaning.

Celestial bodies, such as the sun, moon and stars, are visible.

Synonym clue

A similar word appears nearby.

The task was arduous, or extremely difficult.

Antonym clue

A contrast helps reveal the meaning.

Unlike his gregarious brother, he was quiet and solitary.

Cause-and-effect clue

The result reveals the word’s meaning.

The road was impassable; therefore, the vehicles could not cross it.

Inference clue

The situation gives an indirect clue.

After walking for ten hours without water, the hikers were parched.

3. Step-by-Step Context Method

  1. Read the whole sentence.
  2. Read the sentence before and after it.
  3. Identify the part of speech of the unknown word.
  4. Look for definition, example, contrast, cause or result.
  5. Replace the unknown word with a possible meaning.
  6. Check whether the sentence still makes logical sense.
  7. Choose the most precise option, not merely a possible option.

4. Parts of Speech as a Meaning Clue

7. Contextual Replacement Test

Sentence: “The scientist’s explanation was plausible because it matched the available evidence.”
Inference: Plausible means believable or reasonable, not necessarily proven.
Roman Urdu: Replacement test karein: unknown word ki jagah option rakh kar sentence dobara parhein. Agar meaning, grammar aur logic teenon fit hon, option strong hai.

8. Advanced Context Traps

  • Choosing a broad synonym when the sentence requires a precise one.
  • Ignoring negative words such as not, rarely, hardly, scarcely.
  • Confusing literal and figurative meanings.
  • Ignoring tone, register or emotional attitude.
  • Choosing a meaning that fits the word generally but not this sentence.

Outcome 1.3 — Language, Senses and Figurative Meaning

1. What Is Figurative Language?

Figurative language uses words beyond their literal or ordinary meaning to create imagery, emotion, emphasis, comparison or a deeper idea.

Literal

“The child is cold.”

The child has a low body temperature or feels cold.

Figurative

“Her welcome was cold.”

The welcome was unfriendly or lacking warmth.

Roman Urdu: Literal mein lafz ka seedha meaning hota hai. Figurative mein writer lafz ko image, comparison ya feeling create karne ke liye use karta hai.

4. How to Analyze a Writer’s Language

  1. Identify: What exact word, phrase or device is used?
  2. Classify: Is it a simile, metaphor, personification, imagery, etc.?
  3. Sense: Which sense is activated: sight, sound, touch, smell or taste?
  4. Meaning: What literal idea is being suggested?
  5. Effect: What mood, image, emotion or emphasis does it create?
  6. Purpose: Why might the writer have chosen this expression?
Analysis example: “The moon watched silently over the sleeping village.”
Device: Personification.
Visual element: The moon above the village.
Human quality: Watching silently.
Effect: Creates a calm, protective and slightly mysterious atmosphere.

6. Figurative Language: Common Confusions

  • Simile vs metaphor: Simile normally uses like or as; metaphor makes a direct comparison.
  • Personification vs metaphor: Personification specifically gives human qualities to a non-human entity.
  • Imagery vs figurative device: Imagery is sensory description; a metaphor or simile may be used to create it.
  • Hyperbole vs literal statement: Hyperbole is intentionally excessive and not meant to be measured literally.
  • Alliteration vs rhyme: Alliteration repeats initial consonant sounds; rhyme usually concerns ending sounds.
Analysis warning: Do not identify a device without explaining its effect. “This is a metaphor” is classification only. A complete answer explains what the metaphor suggests and how it affects the reader.

Integrated Passage Analysis

“At dawn, the exhausted hikers entered a silent forest. Wet leaves glittered beneath the pale light, while a cold wind whispered through the branches. Although the path was difficult, the distant scent of pine gave them renewed courage.”
QuestionAnalysis
Which word suggests tiredness?Exhausted. Context: the hikers have been travelling and the path is difficult.
What type of imagery is “wet leaves glittered”?Visual imagery because it appeals to sight.
What device appears in “the wind whispered”?Personification because wind is given a human action.
What does “renewed courage” suggest?The scent of pine psychologically encouraged the hikers to continue.
What mood is created overall?A mixture of fatigue, quiet mystery, natural beauty and eventual hope.

Final High-Yield Revision Sheet

  • Scanning: find a specific detail.
  • Skimming: understand the overall idea.
  • Context: use surrounding words, examples, contrasts and logic.
  • Strong clue types: definition, example, synonym, antonym, cause-effect and inference.
  • Replacement test: put the possible meaning into the sentence.
  • Literal: direct meaning. Figurative: implied or imaginative meaning.
  • Imagery: appeals to sight, sound, touch, smell, taste or movement.
  • Analysis formula: device → sensory appeal → implied meaning → effect → writer’s purpose.
  • MCQ rule: choose textual evidence, not assumptions.
